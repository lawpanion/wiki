THE PROOF OF INTENTION IN CRIMINAL LAW

By Eno · 25 November 2026 · 20 min read

criminal lawcriminal


## **1.0 INTRODUCTION**
Intention is one of the most important elements in the determination of criminal responsibility. In criminal law, the commission of a prohibited act does not, in every circumstance, automatically establish the guilt of the person who committed it. The law generally requires proof that the accused person possessed the particular state of mind required by the offence.
This mental element is known as *mens rea*, while the physical element of the offence is referred to as *actus reus*. The two concepts are important because criminal responsibility ordinarily involves the relationship between what a person does and the state of mind with which the act is done.
The difficulty, however, is that intention is a mental condition. It cannot ordinarily be seen, touched or physically examined. A court cannot open the mind of an accused person to discover what he intended at the time of committing an offence. It must therefore determine intention from the available evidence.
This raises an important question: **How does a court establish what was going on in the mind of an accused person at the time of committing an offence?**
The answer lies principally in the examination of the accused person's conduct, the surrounding circumstances, the nature of the act committed and the consequences that would ordinarily follow from such an act.
In determining intention, two important approaches are recognised: the subjective test and the objective test. Although they approach the question from different perspectives, both are relevant to understanding how intention is established in criminal proceedings.
This article examines the meaning of intention, the differences between the subjective and objective tests, the methods through which intention is proved and the relevant judicial principles under Nigerian criminal law. Particular attention is given to the selected authorities of *Nungu v R*, *Ola v State* and *Orubo v State*.

## **2.0 THE MEANING OF INTENTION IN CRIMINAL LAW**


Intention refers to the state of mind of a person who deliberately seeks to bring about a particular consequence or performs an act with the purpose or knowledge required by law.
In simple terms, intention concerns what a person means to do or what consequence he consciously seeks to bring about.
For example, where A picks up a knife and deliberately stabs B in the chest intending to kill him, A's intention is to cause the death of B.
However, intention must be distinguished from motive.
**Intention** concerns the result a person seeks to achieve, whereas **motive** concerns the reason behind the person's conduct.
For example, a person may kill another because of jealousy, revenge, anger or financial gain. These circumstances may explain the motive for the killing, but the intention concerns whether the accused meant to kill or cause the particular harm required by the offence.
The distinction is important because the existence of a motive does not automatically establish the necessary intention. Equally, the absence of an apparent motive does not necessarily mean that the accused lacked intention.
Under Nigerian criminal law, intention must be considered in relation to the particular offence charged. The prosecution must prove the precise mental element required by the applicable law.
## **3.0 THE SUBJECTIVE TEST OF INTENTION**
The subjective test is concerned with the actual state of mind of the accused person at the time the alleged offence was committed.
It asks what the particular accused person actually intended, knew, believed or foresaw.
The test is therefore personal to the accused. It does not begin by asking what an ordinary reasonable person would have thought or intended. Rather, it seeks to discover the mental condition of the individual standing trial.
For example, suppose A pushes B during an argument. B falls, sustains a serious injury and dies.
The subjective approach requires the court to investigate whether A intended to kill B, intended to cause grievous bodily harm, intended merely to push B away or acted without the intention required for the offence charged.
The fact that B died does not, by itself, establish that A intended his death.
The court may consider A's statements, previous threats, conduct before and after the incident, and other available evidence in determining his actual state of mind.
The subjective test is therefore important because criminal liability should not be imposed merely because an accused person's conduct appears unreasonable to an ordinary person. Where intention is an ingredient of the offence, the prosecution must establish the intention required by law.
## **4.0 THE OBJECTIVE TEST OF INTENTION**
The objective test approaches the determination of intention by examining the accused person's conduct from the standpoint of an ordinary reasonable person placed in similar circumstances.
It considers the nature of the act, the manner in which it was performed and the consequences that would ordinarily be expected to follow from that conduct.
For example, where A repeatedly strikes B on the head with a heavy iron rod, the court may consider whether an ordinary reasonable person would appreciate that such conduct is likely to cause grievous bodily harm or death.
The court may use the nature of the weapon, the force employed and the part of the body targeted to draw an inference about A's intention.
It is important to understand that the objective test does not mean that the court should convict an accused person simply because a reasonable person would have foreseen the consequence.
Rather, objective circumstances provide evidence from which the court may determine the accused person's actual intention where the offence requires proof of that mental element.
### **4.1 The importance of the reasonable-person standard**
The reasonable-person standard provides an external measure for assessing conduct.
It helps the court determine whether the conduct of the accused was such that a particular consequence would ordinarily have been anticipated.
This is particularly useful where there is no direct evidence of the accused person's intention.
Nevertheless, the court must be careful not to confuse what a reasonable person would have intended with what the accused actually intended where the applicable offence requires subjective intention.
## **5.0 THE DIFFERENCES BETWEEN THE OBJECTIVE AND SUBJECTIVE TESTS**
The distinction between the objective and subjective tests is better understood by examining the question each test seeks to answer.
The subjective test asks: *What was actually going on in the mind of the accused person?*
The objective test asks: *What would an ordinary reasonable person have understood, anticipated or foreseen in the same circumstances?*
The following comparison illustrates their differences:
| Subjective test | Objective test || ------------------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- || Examines the actual state of mind of the accused. | Examines conduct against an external reasonable-person standard. || Considers what the accused actually intended. | Considers what a reasonable person would ordinarily foresee. || Focuses on personal knowledge, belief and intention. | Focuses on observable conduct and surrounding circumstances. || Takes account of the accused's actual mental state. | Uses an external standard to assess the conduct. || Relevant where the offence requires actual intention or knowledge. | Particularly relevant where the law requires an objective assessment, such as the reasonable-person element of certain defences. |
### **5.1 Can the two tests operate together?**
Yes. The tests may operate together in determining criminal responsibility, although they are conceptually different.
For example, where an accused person strikes another person repeatedly with a dangerous weapon, the court may objectively examine the weapon, the force used and the part of the body attacked. Those circumstances may then assist the court in determining the accused person's actual intention.
Thus, the objective circumstances may provide evidence of the subjective state of mind.
However, an important distinction must be maintained: evidence that a reasonable person would have foreseen a consequence does not automatically establish that the accused actually possessed the intention required by a particular offence.
### **5.2 The application of the objective test to accident**
The objective test also has a particular application under section 24 of the Criminal Code Act, which deals with events occurring by accident.
The question is whether the event was an accident within the meaning of the law, taking into consideration the standard of an ordinary reasonable and prudent person.
This is different from simply asking whether the accused intended to perform the physical act. A person may deliberately perform an act without intending the particular event that follows from it.
This distinction is important in understanding the relationship between intention and accident.
## **6.0 THE JUDICIAL APPROACH TO PROVING INTENTION**
### **6.1 Nungu v R (1953) 14 WACA 379**
This case provides a useful illustration of how intention may be inferred from the nature of an accused person's conduct and the natural and probable consequences of that conduct.
**Facts of the case**
The appellant, Maye Nungu, had a disagreement with his younger brother over the burning of some trees on the appellant's farm.
During a subsequent exchange of words, the younger brother told the appellant that he had provided the money which enabled the appellant to marry.
The appellant became angry. He picked up an axe and struck his younger brother on the head with the wooden portion of the axe handle, rather than its sharp cutting edge.
The blow inflicted a serious wound, approximately two inches long and one and a half inches wide. The younger brother died almost immediately.
The appellant was subsequently convicted of murder. He appealed against the conviction, arguing, among other things, that he did not intend to kill or cause grievous bodily harm to his brother. He also relied on provocation.
**Issue for determination**
The important question was whether the circumstances of the attack established the necessary intention to cause grievous bodily harm, notwithstanding the fact that the appellant had used the wooden portion of the axe rather than its sharp edge.
Another question was whether the words spoken by the deceased brother amounted to sufficient provocation to reduce the offence from murder to manslaughter.
**Decision of the court**
The West African Court of Appeal dismissed the appeal.
The court held that although the appellant had deliberately turned away the sharp cutting edge of the axe, he must have intended to cause grievous bodily harm by striking his brother on the head with the heavy wooden portion of the axe, which was weighted with an iron head.
The court further held that the deceased's statement concerning the money he had provided for the appellant's marriage did not amount to sufficient provocation to reduce the offence to manslaughter.
**Legal principle**
The case demonstrates that intention may be inferred from the nature of the weapon used, the manner in which it was used, the part of the body targeted and the force employed.
The fact that the appellant did not use the sharp edge of the axe did not necessarily mean that he lacked the intention to cause grievous bodily harm.
**Relevance to the objective and subjective tests**
The case is particularly useful in explaining how objective circumstances assist the court in determining subjective intention.
The court did not have direct access to the appellant's mind. It therefore examined his conduct and the probable consequences of striking a person's head with the heavy end of an axe.
From those circumstances, the court inferred that the appellant intended to cause grievous bodily harm.
It is important for students to appreciate that the court was not simply substituting the intention of a reasonable person for that of the accused. Rather, it used the objective circumstances of the attack to determine the intention attributable to the appellant.
The case also demonstrates that the accused's choice of weapon and the manner in which it is used may be more revealing than an unsupported assertion that he did not intend to cause serious injury.

### **6.2 Ola v State (2014) All FWLR (Pt. 752) 1648 at 1664**
This authority is relevant to the ingredients of murder, particularly the requirement that the prosecution establish the intention accompanying the accused person's act.
**Legal background**
In a charge of murder, it is not enough for the prosecution to establish that a person has died and that the accused person's conduct caused the death. The prosecution must also establish the necessary mental element.
The principle for which *Ola v State* is cited is that the prosecution must establish:
1. That the deceased is dead.2. That the death resulted from the act of the accused person.3. That the accused's act was intentional, with knowledge that death or grievous bodily harm was its probable consequence.
**Importance of the decision**
The third ingredient is particularly important to the proof of intention.
It demonstrates that the law distinguishes between causing death and causing death with the necessary intention. The mere fact that an accused person caused another person's death does not, without more, automatically establish murder.
The court must be satisfied that the prosecution has proved the required intention.
**Relevance to the subjective and objective tests**
The case is useful in understanding the relationship between the two tests.
The subjective aspect concerns whether the accused possessed the requisite intention or knowledge. The objective circumstances may assist the court in reaching that conclusion.
For instance, the court may examine the weapon used, the part of the body attacked, the force employed and the circumstances surrounding the incident.
These circumstances may establish that the accused acted with the intention or knowledge required for murder.
The important lesson for students is that intention is an independent ingredient of murder and must be established by evidence. It cannot simply be assumed from the fact of death.
### **6.3 Orubo v State (2021) 16 NWLR (Pt. 1803) 549**
This authority is relevant to the distinction between the formation of a criminal intention and the actual manifestation of that intention through conduct.
**Legal issue**
The principle associated with the case concerns the ingredients required to establish attempted kidnapping.
The law distinguishes between a person who merely forms an intention to commit an offence and a person who takes steps towards carrying out that intention.
**The ingredients of attempted kidnapping**
The prosecution must establish:
1. That the accused intended to kidnap the victim.2. That the accused manifested that intention through an overt act.3. That the accused was unable to complete the intended offence.
**Decision and legal significance**
The significance of the principle is that an intention, standing alone, is not sufficient to establish the offence of attempt.
There must be an outward act demonstrating that the accused has moved beyond the mere formation of an intention and has embarked upon the execution of the intended offence.
For example, a person who merely thinks about kidnapping another person has not, by that thought alone, committed attempted kidnapping. However, where that person takes concrete steps to seize and carry away the intended victim but fails to complete the offence, the question of attempted kidnapping arises.
**Relevance to the objective and subjective tests**
The case provides an important illustration of the relationship between the subjective and objective elements of criminal liability.
The intention to kidnap is subjective because it concerns what the accused actually intended.
The overt act is objective because it concerns the external conduct through which that intention is manifested.
Consequently, the prosecution must establish both the mental intention and the physical manifestation of that intention.
This is an important lesson for students: criminal law does not generally punish a mere thought. Where the offence is an attempt, there must be conduct sufficiently demonstrating the execution of the criminal intention.
## **7.0 HOW THE COURT DETERMINES THE INTENTION OF AN ACCUSED PERSON**
Having examined the judicial authorities, it is necessary to consider the practical methods through which a court determines the intention of an accused person.
The court does not determine intention by speculation or assumption. It examines the evidence presented by the prosecution and considers whether the circumstances establish the required mental element beyond reasonable doubt.
The following factors are particularly important.
### **7.1 The nature of the weapon used**
The nature of the weapon employed by an accused person may provide evidence of his intention.
For example, a person who deliberately uses a firearm, a knife or a heavy iron object against another person may be understood to appreciate the serious consequences of such conduct.
However, the mere possession or use of a dangerous weapon does not automatically establish an intention to kill. The court must consider the circumstances in which the weapon was used.
This principle is illustrated by *Nungu v R*, where the court considered the use of the wooden portion of an axe and the serious injury inflicted on the deceased.
### **7.2 The part of the body targeted**
The part of the body attacked is another important consideration.
An attack deliberately directed at vital parts of the human body, such as the head, chest or abdomen, may support an inference that the accused intended to cause serious bodily harm.
The reason is that these parts of the body contain vital organs, and an attack directed at them may reasonably be expected to produce serious consequences.
### **7.3 The degree of force employed**
The degree of force used by the accused may also assist the court.
For instance, repeatedly striking a person with a heavy object may indicate an intention to cause serious injury, particularly where the accused continues the attack despite the victim's apparent helplessness.
The court must consider the degree of force in the context of the entire incident.
### **7.4 Previous threats and declarations**
Statements made by the accused before the commission of an offence may provide evidence of intention.
For example, where an accused person threatens to kill another individual and subsequently carries out an attack consistent with that threat, the previous declaration may assist the court in determining intention.
Nevertheless, a threat alone does not establish that the accused committed the subsequent offence. It must be considered alongside the other evidence.
### **7.5 The conduct of the accused after the commission of the offence**
The conduct of an accused person after an incident may also be relevant.
For example, attempts to conceal the weapon used, destroy evidence or escape from the scene may be considered by the court.
However, such conduct must not automatically be treated as proof of intention. The court must examine its relevance to the particular offence and consider all the surrounding circumstances.
### **7.6 The surrounding circumstances**
Perhaps the most important consideration is the totality of the circumstances surrounding the commission of the offence.
The court should not isolate one particular fact and use it as the sole basis for determining intention.
It should consider the events leading to the incident, the relationship between the parties, the manner in which the offence was committed and the conduct of the accused before, during and after the incident.
The ultimate question is whether the totality of the evidence establishes the intention required by law.
## **8.0 THE MAXIMS GOVERNING THE PROOF OF INTENTION**
There are important legal maxims that assist in understanding the relationship between the physical and mental elements of criminal responsibility.
### **8.1 Actus non facit reum nisi mens sit rea**
This Latin maxim means:
*An act does not make a person guilty unless the mind is also guilty.*
It expresses the fundamental principle that criminal responsibility ordinarily requires the coexistence of the prohibited act and the requisite guilty state of mind.
For example, where a person causes the death of another, the prosecution must establish the mental element required for the particular homicide offence before a conviction for that offence can be secured.
The maxim therefore reminds students that the physical commission of an offence and the mental element accompanying it are distinct matters that must be considered together.
However, the principle is subject to statutory exceptions, including offences for which the law does not require proof of intention.
### **8.2 The presumption that a person intends the natural and probable consequences of his acts**
Another important principle is that a person may be presumed to intend the natural and probable consequences of his voluntary acts.
This principle is particularly useful where there is no direct evidence of the accused person's intention.
For instance, where a person deliberately strikes another on the head with a heavy iron object, the court may infer that the accused intended to cause serious bodily harm from the nature of the act and its probable consequences.
This principle is illustrated in *Nungu v R*.
The court considered the nature of the weapon, the part of the body targeted and the force employed before concluding that the appellant must have intended to cause grievous bodily harm.
It is important to understand that this is an evidential inference and not an irrebuttable rule. The court must still consider the totality of the evidence and any explanation or defence raised by the accused.
### **8.3 Cogitationis poenam nemo patitur**
This maxim means:
*No person is punished for mere thoughts.*
The principle establishes that the mere formation of a criminal intention does not ordinarily constitute a criminal offence.
A person may think about committing an offence, plan to commit it or even express an intention to commit it. However, the law generally requires some legally recognised conduct before criminal liability arises.
This principle is particularly relevant to attempted offences.
In *Orubo v State*, the distinction between intention and its manifestation through an overt act is important in understanding the requirements of attempted kidnapping.
Thus, criminal law distinguishes between the intention to commit an offence and the actual commencement of its execution.
## **9.0 THE BURDEN OF PROVING INTENTION IN NIGERIAN CRIMINAL LAW**
Under Nigerian criminal law, the burden of proving the guilt of an accused person rests on the prosecution.
Section 36(5) of the Constitution of the Federal Republic of Nigeria 1999, as amended, provides for the presumption of innocence.
Similarly, section 135 of the Evidence Act 2011 requires proof beyond reasonable doubt where the commission of a crime is directly in issue.
Consequently, where intention is an essential ingredient of an offence, the prosecution must establish that intention beyond reasonable doubt.
The prosecution may establish intention through:
1. Direct evidence, including relevant admissions and confessional statements.2. Circumstantial evidence arising from the conduct of the accused.3. The nature of the weapon used.4. The part of the body targeted.5. The force employed.6. The surrounding circumstances of the incident.
It is important to emphasise that the burden does not shift to the accused person to prove that he lacked intention.
The prosecution must establish the ingredients of the offence charged. Where the evidence raises a reasonable doubt concerning the required intention, the accused person is entitled to the benefit of that doubt.
## **10.0 SUMMARY FOR LAW STUDENTS**
The following points should be remembered when answering examination questions on the proof of intention.
| Concept | What the student should understand || --------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- || Mens rea | The mental element of a criminal offence. || Actus reus | The physical element of a criminal offence. || Subjective test | Examines the actual state of mind of the accused. || Objective test | Examines conduct against an external reasonable-person standard. || Natural and probable consequences | Circumstances from which intention may be inferred. || Overt act | External conduct demonstrating the manifestation of an intention to commit an offence. || Motive | The reason behind the accused person's conduct. || Intention | The purpose or mental state required by the particular offence. |
**Remember these three judicial lessons:**
- *Nungu v R*: The nature of the weapon, the force employed and the part of the body targeted may assist the court in inferring intention.- *Ola v State*: The prosecution must establish the necessary ingredients of murder, including the required intention.- *Orubo v State*: In attempted kidnapping, the prosecution must establish intention and its manifestation through an overt act, together with the failure to complete the intended offence.
## **11.0 CONCLUSION**
The proof of intention is an essential aspect of criminal adjudication because criminal responsibility ordinarily requires more than the establishment of a prohibited physical act.
The subjective test focuses on the actual state of mind of the accused person, while the objective test examines the accused person's conduct against an external standard of reasonableness. Although these tests are different, they may complement each other in determining criminal responsibility.
The objective circumstances surrounding an offence may provide the evidence from which the court infers the subjective intention of the accused. Such circumstances include the nature of the weapon used, the part of the body targeted, the force employed and the events surrounding the commission of the offence.
The decision in *Nungu v R* illustrates how intention may be inferred from the nature and consequences of an accused person's conduct. *Ola v State* demonstrates the importance of establishing the mental ingredient of murder, while *Orubo v State* illustrates the distinction between forming an intention and manifesting that intention through an overt act.
Ultimately, intention must be established through credible evidence and not mere speculation. The prosecution must prove the particular intention required by the offence beyond reasonable doubt before criminal responsibility can properly be imposed.
The fundamental principle remains that the law is concerned not merely with what an accused person has done, but also with the legally relevant state of mind accompanying that conduct.
